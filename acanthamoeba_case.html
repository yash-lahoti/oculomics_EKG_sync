Risk Factor Deep Dive

Soft contact lenses create microabrasions in the corneal epithelium, providing entry points for Acanthamoeba trophozoites. Extended wear dramatically increases risk by reducing tear clearance and increasing hypoxic stress.

Key risk behaviors:

  • Swimming, showering, or surfing while wearing lenses
  • Rinsing lens case or lenses with tap water (not sterile saline)
  • "Topping off" solution rather than replacing daily
  • Not replacing lens case monthly
  • Sleeping in lenses (even occasionally)
Evidence Level: HIGH — Multiple prospective case-control studies confirm CL wear as the dominant risk factor in developed countries.

Acanthamoeba is ubiquitous — found in tap water, soil, and natural bodies of water. Municipal water treatment does not reliably eliminate cysts (chlorination-resistant). Hot tubs and swimming pools are especially high-risk due to aerosolized water.

In Jordan's case: surfing in ocean water with lenses in is the likely inoculation event. Marine environments contain Acanthamoeba and salt water does not sterilize lenses. Rinsing the lens case with tap water further compounds the risk.

Evidence Level: HIGH — Water exposure is the primary transmission route; tap water rinsing is an independent risk factor.

Topical corticosteroids given for misdiagnosed "herpetic" keratitis can catastrophically accelerate AK. Steroids promote cyst-to-trophozoite transition, suppress the host leukocyte response, and are associated with poorer outcomes and higher rates of surgical intervention.

Clinical Pearl: A patient on steroids with presumed "HSV keratitis" not improving should prompt urgent cornea referral and suspension of steroids if AK is suspected.
Evidence Level: HIGH — Steroid use is an independent predictor of worse visual outcomes and need for keratoplasty.

HIV, solid organ transplant recipients, and patients on systemic immunosuppressants can develop AK without lens wear. In these populations, AK can be bilateral and follows a more fulminant course. Jordan has no systemic immunosuppression — an important point suggesting a better baseline prognosis.

Evidence Level: MODERATE — Case reports and series; less well-quantified than CL risk.

Clinical Decision Point 2 of 4

What is the most appropriate next diagnostic test to perform in clinic today?

Correct. In vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM) is the diagnostic test of choice for suspected AK when available. It is non-invasive, provides real-time corneal imaging, and can visualize hyperreflective double-walled Acanthamoeba cysts (15–30 µm) in the epithelium or stroma. Sensitivity approaches 90% in experienced hands.
Reference: Nasit M, Pahuja S. Confocal microscopy in Acanthamoeba keratitis. Indian J Ophthalmol. 2017;65(11):1119–1126.
Incorrect. Standard blood/chocolate agar cultures are for bacterial pathogens. Acanthamoeba requires specialized culture on non-nutrient agar seeded with E. coli. However, confocal microscopy is preferred as the immediate in-office test given its non-invasive nature and high sensitivity.
Incorrect. Anterior segment OCT can define the depth and extent of stromal disease, useful for staging and surgical planning, but cannot identify Acanthamoeba cysts or trophozoites and thus does not establish the diagnosis.
Incorrect. Serum Acanthamoeba antibody titers are not used in clinical diagnosis of AK. Antibodies to Acanthamoeba are found in most healthy adults (environmental exposure is common) and cannot distinguish active keratitis from prior environmental exposure.

Monitor closely at each visit for: recurrence of pain, increase in infiltrate size/density, development of ring infiltrate, and medication toxicity (superficial punctate keratopathy from biguanide toxicity).

Continue PHMB 0.02% BID + propamidine 0.1% BID for a minimum of 12 months. Total duration often extends to 14–18 months in intermediate-stage disease.

Why so long? Acanthamoeba cysts are highly resistant to anti-amoebic drugs. Trophozoites die rapidly, but cysts can persist in the stroma for months. Premature discontinuation risks trophozoite re-emergence from dormant cysts — a leading cause of clinical relapse.

Steroid controversy: Low-dose topical steroids may be introduced in late disease to suppress stromal inflammation after infection is confirmed quiescent (typically >3 months of treatment, with negative repeat IVCM). This requires expert judgment and close IOP monitoring.

Clinical Decision Point 4 of 4

Jordan asks: "Why can't you just give me antibiotic drops that work for other eye infections?" Which statement best explains the therapeutic rationale?

Correct. Acanthamoeba is a free-living protozoan — bacterial antibiotics are entirely ineffective. PHMB and chlorhexidine (biguanides) are cationic antiseptics that bind and disrupt phospholipid cell membranes of trophozoites and cysts. Diamidines (propamidine, hexamidine) enter the cell and disrupt DNA synthesis. Dual therapy targets both life-cycle forms synergistically and has no equivalent in bacterial antibiotic pharmacology.
Reference: Dart JK, Saw VP, Kilvington S. Acanthamoeba Keratitis: Diagnosis and Treatment Update 2009. Am J Ophthalmol. 2009;148(4):487–499.
Incorrect. Standard antibiotics have no activity against Acanthamoeba — not "partial" activity. Acanthamoeba is a protozoan (eukaryote); fluoroquinolones and other antibiotics target bacterial mechanisms (DNA gyrase, cell wall synthesis) that do not exist in eukaryotic protozoa.
Incorrect. Acanthamoeba is a protozoan (eukaryote), not a fungus. Voriconazole has been investigated as an adjunct in refractory AK but is not primary therapy. Standard antifungals (natamycin, fluconazole) have no meaningful activity against Acanthamoeba.
Incorrect. Metronidazole targets anaerobic bacteria and certain gut protozoa but has no established efficacy against Acanthamoeba keratitis. The cornea is avascular — systemic therapy does not adequately penetrate it. AK treatment is primarily topical.

Complications, Scarring & Follow-Up

Jordan responds to treatment over the next 3 months — pain resolves, IVCM shows decreasing cyst density. However, he develops anterior stromal scarring as the infiltrate resolves. At 18 months post-diagnosis, he has a central scar affecting vision (BCVA 20/200 OD). A discussion of surgical options is initiated.

Anterior stromal scarring is the most common residual complication after AK, resulting from trophozoite-mediated proteolysis and collagen disruption. Central scars cause irregular astigmatism and vision loss. Prognosis correlates with depth and centrality of scarring.

Management options:

  • Superficial scar + normal posterior stroma: RGP or scleral lens to optimize optics
  • Deep stromal scar / poor BCVA: Penetrating Keratoplasty (PKP) or Deep Anterior Lamellar Keratoplasty (DALK) after quiescence
  • Surgical timing: minimum 3–6 months after confirmed quiescence; ideally 12+ months
Jordan's outcome: Central stromal scar (2.5 mm), BCVA 20/200. RGP trial unsuccessful due to irregular topography. Referral for PKP at 18 months post-diagnosis.

Teaching Points & Key Takeaways

1. Pain >> Signs = Think AK
Disproportionate pain (especially burning, photophobia) before a striking slit-lamp infiltrate is the early AK hallmark. This distinguishes it from bacterial keratitis, where pain is proportional to severity.
2. CL + Water = AK Until Proven Otherwise
Any CL wearer with keratitis and history of water exposure (pool, ocean, tap water rinsing) should have AK on the immediate differential — do not wait for antiviral failure.
3. Perineural Infiltrates Are Pathognomonic
White infiltrates tracking the corneal nerve plexus radially are virtually diagnostic of AK when present. They are present in ~63% of early cases and may paradoxically regress as disease advances.
4. IVCM First, Scrape to Confirm
Confocal microscopy is fast, non-invasive, and sensitive (~90%). Follow with corneal scrape for PCR and culture to establish definitive diagnosis, especially if treatment response is poor.
5. Never Give Steroids Early in AK
Topical corticosteroids promote trophozoite growth, suppress immune response, and dramatically worsen outcomes. Steroid use is an independent predictor of need for keratoplasty.
6. Treat for 12–18 Months
Cysts are drug-resistant. A prolonged course (minimum 12 months) is required to eliminate the cyst reservoir and prevent relapse. Premature discontinuation is a leading cause of recurrence.
7. PKP Outcomes Are Good if Timed Correctly
PKP for post-AK scarring after quiescence achieves 70% graft survival at 4 years. Performing PKP in active disease carries higher recurrence risk. LLKP is an emerging option for early refractory cases.
8. Prevent With Patient Education
Never swim/shower in CL; use sterile saline, not tap water; replace cases monthly; do not "top off" solution; remove daily. These reduce AK risk substantially.
